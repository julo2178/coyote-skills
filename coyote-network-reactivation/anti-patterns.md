# Anti-patterns — what kills warm reconnection DMs

These are the failure modes, ranked by severity. Each shows a concrete bad example next to the corrected version so the user can see the pattern.

## 1. Generic opener (severity: critical)

The contact pattern-matches to bulk DM within 5 seconds and deletes. This is the single most common failure.

**Bad**
> Hi Sarah, hope you're doing well! It's been a while since we last connected. Just wanted to reach out and reconnect.

**Good**
> Hi Sarah, your name came up the other day when I was thinking about that nightmare migration weekend in 2019. Honestly still one of the cleaner war-room operations I've ever been part of, mostly because of how you handled the rollback decision.

The anchor is specific (the migration weekend), the credit is specific (the rollback decision), and the contact knows immediately this is a real memory, not a template.

## 2. Mission / job mention in opener (severity: critical)

Turns the message into a transactional ask the moment the contact reads it.

**Bad**
> Hi Marc, long time no see! I wanted to reach out because I'm currently looking for a new senior product role and I thought you might know of opportunities at [current company].

**Good**
> Hi Marc, I was reviewing some old roadmap docs from the [project] days and ended up rereading the architecture decision you forced through against half the room in 2021. Aged exceptionally well. Saw you're at [current company] now. Curious how that move's been.

If a contact replies, you can mention what you're up to factually in the second exchange. Not in the opener.

## 3. CV, Calendly or portfolio link attached (severity: critical)

Even one link kills the warmth instantly. The opener is text only. Period.

**Bad**
> Hi Anna, would love to reconnect. Here's my updated profile in case useful: linkedin.com/in/...

**Good**
> Hi Anna, [specific anchor], [low-pressure close]. (No links, no attachments, nothing.)

If they reply asking what you're doing now, you can share a link in the third or fourth exchange. The opener is signal-only.

## 4. "Quick coffee?" close (severity: high)

Implies obligation, low reply rate. The contact mentally calculates the calendar cost and either ghosts or politely declines.

**Bad**
> Would love to grab a quick coffee soon!

**Good**
> Would love to hear what you're up to.

The first asks for time. The second asks for an update. The second is much easier to say yes to.

## 5. Apologising for losing touch (severity: medium)

Lowers your status. The contact reads a slightly self-flagellating tone and the message starts on the back foot.

**Bad**
> So sorry I dropped off the radar after I left [company]! I should have stayed in touch.

**Good**
> (Skip the apology entirely, or state it neutrally: "It's been a while.")

## 6. Long monologue about the user's life (severity: medium)

The contact didn't ask. One sentence max about you in the opener, the rest is about them or the shared past.

**Bad**
> Hi James! Quick update on me: I left [company] in 2022, did a brief stint at [other company], started consulting in 2023, then took a sabbatical, now I'm building a small AI advisory practice. Anyway, how are you?

**Good**
> Hi James, [specific anchor about James]. [Low-pressure question about James]. (One sentence max about you, only if it's directly relevant.)

## 7. Vague anchor (severity: medium)

A weak version of #1. The user references a memory but it's not specific enough to feel real.

**Bad**
> Hi Léa, thinking back to our days at [company]. Good times.

**Good**
> Hi Léa, thinking back to that morning we redesigned the entire onboarding flow on a whiteboard before the 10am exec review. Still use that framework with new teams.

The first is generic nostalgia. The second proves you actually remember.

## 8. Asking the contact to do something for you (severity: high)

Even an indirect ask poisons the opener. "Would you mind making an intro to..." is a no-no. Save asks for after the relationship is re-established.

**Bad**
> Hi Tom, would you mind introducing me to [person] at [company]? I saw you're connected.

**Good**
> Hi Tom, [specific anchor], [low-pressure close]. (Send the intro ask 3-4 messages later, once warmth is re-established.)

## 9. Multiple anchor points (severity: low)

Trying to reference two or three shared memories in the same opener dilutes the signal. One specific anchor is stronger than three vague ones.

**Bad**
> Hi Sophie, thinking back to the 2018 launch, the way you handled the partnerships team, that conference we both spoke at, and the time we...

**Good**
> Hi Sophie, [one specific anchor with depth].

## 10. Forcing the FR/EN choice wrong (severity: medium)

If the contact is bilingual but the original working language was French, sending in English reads as oddly distant. Match the language of your original relationship, not the language of where they live now.

**Bad**
> [Sending an English DM to an ex-Alcatel-Lucent colleague who you only ever worked with in French]

**Good**
> [Send in French. Switch to English only if they reply in English.]

---

## When to skip the contact entirely

If you can't pass these checks, don't send the DM. Mark them `❌ Out of reach` in the tracker.

- You can't recall a single specific shared moment
- The original relationship was minimal (you both attended the same all-hands, never actually worked together)
- The contact left the industry and is in a completely unrelated field
- You can't write the message without an underlying ask
- You haven't spoken in 15+ years and the relationship was already weak at the time

There's no shame in marking a contact out of reach. A short, qualified list of warm DMs beats a long list of cold ones.
